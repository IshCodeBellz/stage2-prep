How the testing and the interviews will go

This is the shape of the talk-through, in the order you will meet it. On the call it is fitted to your operator and your invite letter — every operator runs the process slightly differently, and where your letter disagrees with anything here, the letter wins.

1. The application and the online tests

The form is a sift, and so are the unsupervised tests many operators attach to it: usually a situational judgement exercise, sometimes a concentration or visual search task, occasionally numerical reasoning. We go through what your operator's version asks, why a big screen and a quiet hour matter, and which of these count against your attempts.

2. The invite and the week before

What your invite letter is telling you — RAAT, SCAAT, "Vienna tests", Stage 1 and Stage 2 all mean different things at different operators. The practice pack you are entitled to at least a week beforehand, and how to use it. Adjustments, if you need them, and why the request has to go in early. What to take, what to wear, and when to stop practising.

3. The assessment centre, in order

We walk the day as it will run: arrival and ID, the briefing, then the tests one at a time. For each one — what the administrator reads out, what the practice items look like, what the test is really measuring, and the mistake that costs most people marks on it.

  • The attention and rules tests. Group Bourdon, TEA-Occ, TRP1b, TRP2 and the DFFT. Timed papers where pace and errors are scored together, and where most first-sitting losses are people working at the wrong speed.
  • The Vienna tests. At a machine with a response panel: the thirty-minute WAFV vigilance test, ATAVT, 2HAND, and at some operators the Determination Test. Different skills, and a different kind of tired.
  • Situational judgement. Ranking and rating responses to workplace scenarios, and the safety-first order that settles most of them.

We also talk about the parts that are not tests: the warm room, the long afternoon, the pencils around you that sound faster than yours, and the fact that nobody tells you how you did before you go home.

4. The MMI

The structured interview at the assessment centre, fed by a form you fill in beforehand with written examples — commonly around six. We look at which examples from your working life to use, how they will be probed two or three questions deep, and where answers usually fall apart: the team did it rather than you did it, or the story stops before the part where you checked it worked. If there is time, we run one example as it will be asked.

5. The drivers' manager interview

Usually weeks later, usually at the depot, usually with a driver manager, and at most operators the last thing before a conditional offer. It is conversational rather than scored against a frame, and it weighs different things: whether you understand the roster and what it does to a life, what you know about the operator and the routes, and whether they would put you in a cab. We go through the questions that come up and what a realistic answer sounds like.

6. After the offer

The rail medical — vision, colour vision, hearing, general fitness and a drugs and alcohol screen — then references and background checks, then twelve to eighteen months of paid training. Briefly, so you know what is coming and what would end it.

Before you book

Can you guarantee I will get in?

No, and be careful with anyone who does. Operators set their own cut-off scores, do not publish them, and move them. What a session reliably fixes is surprise — about the form, the tests and the interviews — and the avoidable mistakes that come from it.

Will you tell me what is on the real tests?

No. Nobody outside the assessment providers has the real tests, and anyone claiming to is not someone to take advice from. We talk through the published format of each test, what it measures and how to approach it — the same ground as the guides, fitted to you.

Are you connected to a train operating company?

No. This is independent advice. We are not affiliated with any train operating company, assessment provider or awarding body, and a session has no bearing on any decision an operator makes about you.

What if I need to move it, or it does not help?

Move it free with at least 48 hours' notice; with less, the session counts as used — the rescheduling terms are above. And if you have the call and finish it feeling it was not worth the money, say so within fourteen days and the payment is refunded, the same policy as everything else on the site. That refund is for a session that took place, not one that was missed.

When is the best time to book?

Before your first application, if you can — the CV and the form are where most people are sifted out, and the attempts rule makes the first sitting the expensive one. Otherwise, as soon as the invite arrives, so there are weeks left to act on what we talk about.

I have been through it before and failed. Is it still worth it?

Often more so. Bring whatever feedback you were given and the dates of your earlier sittings, and we will look at what the attempts rule leaves you and where the time before the next one is best spent.
