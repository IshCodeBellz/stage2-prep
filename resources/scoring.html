Reading your own scores

A practice score is only useful if you know what it is telling you. Most people read the headline number, which is the least informative part of it.

platinum All stages 8 min read

Three ideas do most of the work here: your score is a trade-off, it is relative, and one sitting is noise.

Speed and error are one number, not two

On every timed attention test, you are choosing a point on a curve. Push the pace and your error rate rises; protect accuracy and your coverage falls. There is no setting where both improve, which is why "work faster" is not advice.

What practice actually changes is the whole curve — familiarity with the format means that at any given pace, you make fewer errors than you did last week. So the useful question after a sitting is not "was that a good score" but "at this pace, are my errors going up or down over the last five sittings?"

Practical version: find the fastest pace at which your errors stay near zero, and live one notch below it. Most people are two notches above it and losing marks to corrections.

Two kinds of error, treated differently

  • Misses — things you should have marked and did not. Usually a pace or attention problem.
  • False marks — things you marked that you should not have. Usually a carelessness or anxiety problem, and on most keys they cost you more.

If your misses are high, you are going too fast or drifting. If your false marks are high, you are guessing under time pressure. Different diseases, different treatments.

Your score is relative, not absolute

Assessment results are almost always expressed against a norm group — other candidates for the same kind of role. "I got seventy per cent" means nothing without knowing what seventy per cent of the material represents and what everybody else got.

That has two consequences worth internalising. First, a test that feels impossible probably felt impossible to everyone, and adaptive tests like ATAVT and the Determination Test are engineered to feel that way. Second, no one can tell you the pass mark, because it is set by the operator, is not published, and moves with how many people they need. Anyone quoting you a number is selling something.

Compensatory, or must-pass?

Some batteries are scored as a whole, so a strong test can offset a weaker one. Others treat certain tests — usually the ones closest to safety-critical performance, like concentration and vigilance — as thresholds you must clear regardless of everything else. Many operators use a mixture, and few publish which.

The practical implication is one-directional and simple: do not bank on compensation. A profile with one hole in it is the risky one, and the effort is better spent lifting your worst test than polishing your best.

Reading your practice history

  • Use the median of your last five, not your best. Your best sitting is the one where everything went right, and the day will not be that one.
  • Compare the second half of a paper with the first. A consistent fall-off is a stamina finding, not a skill finding, and it is fixed by full-length sittings rather than more drills.
  • Watch variance, not just level. Scores bouncing between very good and poor usually mean an unstable method — you are doing it differently each time. Pick one method and hold it even when it feels slow.
  • Plateau is normal. Attention tests improve quickly for a week or two and then flatten. A plateau at a decent level is not a reason to panic or to change technique.
  • Three sittings before any conclusion. One bad run tells you about last night's sleep. Three tell you about the test.
The history is already there

Every exam-version sitting is kept on your device with the date, so the trend is visible without you writing anything down. Practice runs are deliberately not counted — only the exam versions set a best.

Bad sitting, or real weakness?

Before you conclude that a test is your problem, rule out the four things that produce a bad sitting in someone with no weakness at all:

  1. Sleep. By a distance the largest effect on attention testing, and the easiest to mistake for incompetence.
  2. Time of day. Everyone has a trough. If all your practice is at your best hour and the assessment is at your worst, you have not measured what you think you have.
  3. Novelty. The first two sittings of anything are unrepresentative. Discard them.
  4. Setup. Screen size, distance, glasses, sound. A phone-sized screen changes visual search scores materially.

If the low score survives all four, it is real — and now it is useful, because a known weakness with three weeks left is a plan rather than a surprise.

The attempts you get

Most operators limit how many goes you have and how long you wait between them; two attempts with at least twelve months between is the common pattern, and because some of these instruments are shared across the industry, a recent sitting elsewhere can sometimes count against you. Check your operator's own wording before applying, not after.

This is the entire argument for taking the first attempt seriously. The cost of arriving unprepared is not a wasted morning; it is potentially a year.

What to do with a genuine weakness

  • Do not grind the same test daily for an hour. Diminishing returns set in fast and resentment sets in faster.
  • Diagnose which of the three problems it is — too slow, too many errors, or falling apart late — and use the matching fix: pace work, method work, or full-length stamina sittings.
  • Fix the method before the speed. Speed built on a bad method plateaus early and collapses under pressure.
  • Accept a ceiling if you find one. These tests measure something real. If a test is genuinely not your strength, the right response is to make everything else unambiguous rather than to spend your last week on the one that will not move.
