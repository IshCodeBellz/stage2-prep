TRP2: the order you check things in

A rule you can learn in ten seconds, applied forty-three times in eight minutes. The test is not whether you understand it. It is whether you are still applying it correctly at item thirty-eight.

gold Stage 1 6 min read

You are shown a set of dials. Each has a pointer, and each sits on a background — white, grey or black — and the background sets how urgently that dial needs checking. You give the order you would check them in.

Items
Around forty-three
Clock
Commonly eight minutes, ending hard
Per item
About eleven seconds
Scoring
Correct orderings; consistency across the paper

Why an easy rule is a hard test

Three things make this harder than it reads:

  • The rule is arbitrary. There is no reason white should outrank black. Rules with no logic behind them are exactly the ones that decay under fatigue, which is the point — the railway is full of conventions that are true because they are written down.
  • The pointer is a distractor. Your eye goes to the arrow, because arrows mean something. The arrow direction is not the priority. Every time you catch yourself looking at it first, that is the test working.
  • Eleven seconds is not long. Long enough to do it properly, nowhere near long enough to do it twice.

The method

  1. Say the rule out loud before you start, twice. Whatever your version's priority order is, get it in your mouth rather than your notes. You are going to need it as a reflex, not a lookup.
  2. Read backgrounds only, first pass. Scan the dials for shade and nothing else. Sort them. Then, and only then, look at the pointers if the item needs them.
  3. Answer in one movement. Decide, mark, move. Second-guessing this test is almost always a downgrade — your first read of a shade is right far more often than your re-read, because the re-read is contaminated by the previous item.
  4. Re-anchor every ten items. One second, silently: the rule, in order. Drift on this test is not dramatic; it is a quiet inversion around item twenty-five that you do not notice and that costs you the rest of the paper.
  5. Check your answer row. On a paper answer sheet, glance at the item number every ten. One skipped row turns thirty right answers into thirty wrong ones, and it is the single most catastrophic and most preventable failure on the whole day.
Drill it: TRP2

The exam version runs the full forty-three in eight minutes and ends at zero. The practice version is seven with no clock, which is enough to install the rule before you race it.

Open the drill

Where people lose it

  • Prioritising by pointer. The most natural error there is. The shading decides.
  • Reversing the order halfway. Usually undetected. The fix is the ten-item re-anchor, not concentration.
  • Going back to fix an earlier item. Costs two future items to repair one past one, and leaves you out of position on the answer sheet.
  • Losing the row. Check the number every ten items. Every ten.
  • Slowing down when unsure. Items are not weighted. A fast wrong answer and a slow wrong answer score the same, but the slow one also costs you the next item.

How to practise it

  • Short and frequent. Five minutes a day for a week installs the rule far better than an hour on Sunday.
  • Practise the untimed version once to get the rule, then never again — everything after that should be against the clock, because pace is the whole difficulty.
  • Check your accuracy over the second half of the paper against the first. If the back half is worse, you are drifting, and re-anchoring is the fix.
  • Practise when you are tired, deliberately, at least once. Arbitrary rules fail first when you are tired, and the real paper is rarely the first thing of the morning.
