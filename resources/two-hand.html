2HAND: co-ordination under a moving line

One hand moves the dot sideways, the other moves it up and down, and the channel is narrower than your confidence. It is a co-ordination test with a stress test hidden in it.

gold Stage 2 6 min read

Two controls, each responsible for one axis, and a track you have to stay inside. Contact with the edge is registered, usually with a noise, and the noise is part of the test.

Task
Keep the dot inside the channel from end to end
Controls
One per axis — neither hand can do the job alone
Scoring
Time on track against time and contacts off it
Hidden measure
What happens to your next ten seconds after a mistake

Two things at once, neither of them optional

The difficulty is not manual dexterity. It is that the two hands have to co-operate continuously on a single outcome, and your brain would much rather run them in turns. Watch a beginner and you will see it: a horizontal correction, then a vertical one, then a horizontal one, producing a staircase up a diagonal instead of a line.

The fix is not faster hands. It is learning to make both corrections small and continuous, so neither hand is ever doing much.

The method

  1. Grip lightly. The most common single fault. A tight grip converts a small intended movement into a large actual one and makes fine correction impossible. Your hands should be resting on the controls, not gripping them.
  2. Support your wrists or forearms. Unsupported arms tremble within a minute and the tremble goes straight into the track.
  3. Look ahead of the dot, not at it. Exactly like driving: you steer where you are looking. Watching the dot produces reactive, late corrections. Watching the channel ten per cent ahead produces anticipation, which is what smoothness is made of.
  4. Correct early and small. Two tiny corrections beat one big one, always. Big corrections cause the overshoot that causes the next big correction.
  5. Slow down for corners, deliberately. There is usually no reward for finishing early and there is always a penalty for contact. Go into the tight sections slower than feels necessary.
  6. Absorb the buzzer. A contact is a small deduction. The panic that follows it is a large one. Decide now that when you hear it you will breathe out, keep the same pace, and carry on — and then practise exactly that.
Drill it: 2-Hand

Ten runs in the exam version, six in practice, with a different channel each attempt so you cannot learn one track. A gamepad works if you have one — left stick sideways, right stick up and down.

Open the drill

Where people lose it

  • The death grip. Check your hands halfway through a run. If they are tight, that is where your points went.
  • Chasing the dot. Eyes ahead on the channel, not on the thing you are moving.
  • Alternating axes. The staircase pattern. Both hands, always, softly.
  • Freezing after contact. A stopped dot on the edge accumulates more penalty than a moving one that recovers.
  • Sprinting the last section. The end of the track is where tired hands meet impatience, and it is where most contacts happen.

How to practise it

  • Short sessions, frequently. Motor skills consolidate with rest between sessions far better than with long grinding ones, and tired hands teach bad habits.
  • Do a few runs deliberately slowly, prioritising zero contacts over the clock. Smooth first, then quick. It never works the other way round.
  • Practise with the sound on. The buzzer is part of what you are training against, and a silent practice run does not prepare you for the flinch.
  • Use whatever input you will face if you know it, and if you do not, practise on more than one — the co-ordination transfers, the specific feel of the control does not.
