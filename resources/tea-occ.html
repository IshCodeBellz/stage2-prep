TEA-Occ: counting, searching, and doing both

Three short parts that look like three separate tests. They are not. The first two exist to produce a baseline, and the third is the question.

gold Stage 1 8 min read

TEA-Occ is the occupational version of the Test of Everyday Attention. You count tones while ignoring others; you search a page of directory entries for a symbol; and then you do both at once. It needs working ears and you will normally be given a headset.

Part one
Count target tones, ignore the distractor
Part two
Search a directory page for marked entries
Part three
Both at the same time
Scoring
Accuracy on each, and what part three costs you

The number that matters

Your search rate in part two is your baseline. Your search rate in part three is the same job with a second one running underneath it. The gap between them — the dual-task decrement — is what the test is for, and it is why doing brilliantly on the first two parts and falling apart on the third is a worse result than three merely decent parts.

For a driver the relevance is obvious once you see it. Reading a signal is easy. Reading a signal while the cab radio is talking to you, while you are counting down to a speed restriction you were warned about two minutes ago, is the job.

Part one: counting with something in the way

Target tones and distractor tones are distinguished by pitch. You count one kind and ignore the other. Simple, until a distractor lands in the middle of a run and takes the number with it.

  1. Count on a rhythm, not in words. A steady internal beat that the number rides on is far more robust than saying "seven… eight…" to yourself, because a distractor can interrupt the words but not the beat.
  2. Tune to the target's pitch before the run starts, during the practice items. Decide which pitch is yours and let the other become texture. Most of the damage is done by candidates who are still deciding which is which when the run begins.
  3. Never restart after a distractor. If you feel the number wobble, hold the last one you were sure of and carry on from it. One uncertain count beats an abandoned one.
  4. If you genuinely lose it, give your best estimate. A blank is worth nothing and an estimate is often right.

Part two: the directory search

A page of entries, and a symbol or mark you are looking for. This is a visual search under time, and it rewards the same discipline as Group Bourdon.

  1. Search for the mark, not the entry. Do not read names or numbers. Let your eye look for the shape of the symbol against the page texture.
  2. One column at a time, downwards, place marker down. No zigzagging and no jumping to a promising area.
  3. Do not verify twice. Mark and move.

Part three: both at once

This is where the plan matters, because you cannot give equal attention to both and the test knows it. The order of priority that works:

  • Protect the count. It is the fragile one. A lost count cannot be rebuilt; a slower search is just a slower search.
  • Let the search slow down. Expect it to. Everybody's does, and a controlled twenty per cent drop with accurate counting reads far better than a maintained search rate with a broken count.
  • Do not batch. The temptation is to search hard through a quiet stretch, then stop searching entirely to catch up on counting. Alternating in blocks produces worse results on both than running them together at a slightly reduced pace.
  • Breathe on the beat. It sounds like nonsense and it works: the rhythm that carries the count is the thing that stops the search from stealing it.

Where people lose it

  • Counting on their fingers. Often not permitted, and it makes the count dependent on a hand that the search part wants to use.
  • Restarting the count. The single biggest loss. Carry the uncertain number.
  • Reading the directory entries. You are looking for a symbol. Reading words is a slower channel and it competes directly with the counting.
  • Volume left wrong. Say something before the test starts if the headset is too quiet or the tones are uncomfortable. You will not be allowed to mention it afterwards.
  • Practising in silence. Practise this one with the sound on and, occasionally, somewhere mildly noisy. The real room has other people in it.

How to practise it

  • Run the three parts in order, as designed, rather than drilling the counting alone. The transition into part three is the skill.
  • Record the two search rates each time and watch the gap close. The gap is your progress; the absolute numbers are less interesting.
  • If the gap is not closing, slow your part-two search deliberately. Many people are baselining at a pace they cannot hold with a second task, which makes the decrement look worse than it is.
  • Two or three sittings a week is plenty. This one tires you out faster than it looks.
