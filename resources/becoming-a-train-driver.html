How to become a train driver in the UK

No degree, no rail experience and no car licence required. What it does take is eighteen months of patience, a medical you cannot revise for, and one morning of tests that almost everybody underestimates. Here is the whole route, in order.

starter All stages 12 min read

Driving trains on Britain's mainline railway is one of a handful of jobs with almost no entry qualifications and a queue several hundred deep for every vacancy. Those two facts are the same fact. Because the door is open to anyone, the industry filters at the assessment centre instead — which is why the selection process, not your CV, is what decides this.

The route has seven steps and takes most people between eighteen months and three years from first application to driving a train on their own. Nothing about it is hidden. It is just long, and the bit that eliminates people comes early.

Step 1 — Check you are eligible before you spend a Sunday on the form

There are four hard gates, and three of them are not negotiable by effort: your age, your eyesight and colour vision, your general health, and how far you live from the depot. People routinely get through two rounds of a process they were never going to pass a medical for.

The legal requirements sit in the Train Driving Licences and Certificates Regulations 2010, and they are thinner than the internet suggests: nine years of primary and secondary education, a medical, an occupational psychological assessment, and a professional knowledge exam. Operators then add their own conditions on top — and those are usually what stops people.

The eligibility checklist goes through all of it, including the eyesight numbers and the things people wrongly believe are disqualifying.

Step 2 — Find the vacancy, which is the part that needs patience

Trainee driver vacancies are advertised by individual train operating companies on their own careers pages, and by the freight operators, and by the metros separately. There is no national application. Some operators recruit continuously; most open a window, take a fixed number of applications and close it — sometimes within hours of opening.

  • The Train Drivers Academy — the industry's own front door, run by the Rail Delivery Group. It lists operators by region and points you at their live vacancies. thetraindriversacademy.com
  • The operators directly. Set up job alerts on the careers site of every operator with a depot you could realistically book on at. This is the single highest-value hour you will spend.
  • Freight. DB Cargo, Freightliner, GB Railfreight and others recruit drivers too, often with less competition and more route variety, and usually more antisocial hours.
  • Metros and light rail. London Underground, Merseyrail, the Tyne and Wear Metro and the tram systems run their own processes, outside the mainline licensing regime. The Underground in particular has historically recruited train operators largely from its own station staff.

Depot distance is a real filter, not a formality. Most operators require you to live within a set travelling time of the depot you would book on at, they check it, and they very rarely fund relocation. Apply where you actually are.

Step 3 — The application form, which is where most people are rejected

The form is a screening instrument, not an introduction. It asks for an unbroken employment history, why you want the job, and a set of competency answers about safety, concentration and working alone. Most applications die here, and they die for dull reasons: gaps left unexplained, a word limit ignored, an example that describes what the team did rather than what you did.

Many operators now attach online tests to the application itself — a situational judgement exercise, a concentration or visual search task, sometimes numerical reasoning. These are sat at home, unsupervised, and they are a genuine cut, not a formality.

Applying: windows, forms and the one-at-a-time rule covers the form, the online stage, and the rule about running two applications at once that catches a lot of people out.

Step 4 — The assessment centre

This is the part this site exists for. If you are invited, you will spend somewhere between half a day and a full day sitting a standardised battery of psychometric tests, run either by your operator or by an assessment provider on its behalf.

The battery is not invented by each operator. It is set out in a rail industry standard, RIS-3751-TOM, whose current issue four came into force on 7 December 2024. Appendix A of that standard maps each thing being measured to the test that measures it:

  • Attention — paper Group Bourdon and the Test of Everyday Attention (TEA-Occ)
  • Vigilance — the WAFV vigilance test
  • Memory — TRP1, part 1b
  • Reasoning — TRP2
  • Perception — ATAVT, the adaptive traffic test
  • Reaction time — taken from WAFV and ATAVT
  • Hand co-ordination — 2HAND
  • Communication — the MMI, the multi-modal interview
  • Behavioural aptitudes — the situational judgement exercise and the MMI

That list is the whole assessment centre, and every one of those tests has a guide and a simulator here. Operators may add tests of their own on top, and they set their own pass marks — the standard deliberately does not.

Group Bourdon, TEA-Occ, TRP1b, TRP2, WAFV, ATAVT, 2HAND and the judgement exercises, at the real lengths. Start with the thirty-minute vigilance test; it is the one nobody rehearses.

Step 5 — The interviews, of which there are usually two

Both arrive as acronyms nobody explains:

  • The MMI, the multi-modal interview. The structured one at the assessment centre, named in the standard above, scored against a fixed frame. You complete a form beforehand naming the past situations you intend to talk about, and the questions then go three or four levels into those examples. Examples may come from work, home, education or your interests — the assessors are explicit about that, which matters if your CV is thin. What they will not accept is an answer about what your team did.
  • The DMI, the drivers' manager interview. A separate, longer and much more conversational interview with a manager from the depot you would work at, usually after the assessment centre and before the medical. It is where motivation, company knowledge and whether you have understood the roster actually get weighed, and at most operators it is the last thing between you and a conditional offer.

The interview guide covers both, and the difference in how you prepare for them is larger than people expect.

Step 6 — Medical, references and security checks

The offer is conditional. What follows is a rail medical against the standards in the regulations — vision, colour vision, hearing, general fitness, blood pressure, and a drugs and alcohol screen — plus reference and employment checks, usually covering three to five years, and a criminal record check.

The medical is the only stage you cannot prepare for in the week before, and the only one where a surprise ends the process outright. If there is any doubt about your colour vision, find out from an optician before you apply, not after four months of assessments.

Step 7 — Training, and the licence at the end of it

Trainee drivers are employed and paid from day one. Training typically runs twelve to eighteen months: rules and safety in a classroom, traction courses on each type of train you will drive, simulator work, then many months of practical handling and route learning with a driver instructor beside you.

At the end you hold two things. A train driving licence, issued by the Office of Rail and Road, valid for ten years and portable between employers — it says you are medically and psychologically fit and know the railway in general. And a train driving certificate, issued by your employer, which names the specific traction you may drive and the specific routes you may drive it over. The certificate is why moving operator is not simply a matter of handing in your notice.

Training, licensing and what the job pays goes through the whole of it, including the medicals you keep sitting for the rest of your career.

What the competition actually looks like

Published figures vary from a few hundred applicants per vacancy to several thousand at a popular London depot, and nobody outside the operators has reliable numbers. Treat any specific figure with suspicion, including that range. What is not in dispute is the shape: hundreds of people per post, most eliminated on the form, and the assessment centre doing the rest.

That shape is oddly encouraging. Very little of the filtering is about who you are or where you went to school. Most of it is about whether you can concentrate accurately at pace for a long time — which is a skill, which is trainable, and which is what everything else on this site is for.

The routes in, if the front door is shut

  • Direct application as a trainee driver. The main route, open to anyone eligible, and the most competitive.
  • The Level 3 train driver apprenticeship. The same job with an apprenticeship wrapper; the industry's implementation plan has brought the minimum entry age down to 17 and a half. It usually requires level 2 maths and English, or you study them alongside.
  • From inside the industry. Conductor, guard, station or platform staff, shunter, dispatcher. Internal applicants know the rule book and the culture, often get first sight of vacancies, and at some operators — the Underground especially — this has been the normal route rather than the alternative one.
  • Freight first, passenger later. Less competition to get in, harder hours, and a licence is a licence.

What people get wrong

  • Applying everywhere at once. The industry checks a national database of assessment results. Running several applications in parallel does not multiply your chances; it risks burning your limited attempts at the tests. One at a time, to conclusion.
  • Treating the assessment centre as a formality after a good interview. It is the opposite way round. The tests come first and they are where the process is decided.
  • Waiting to prepare until a vacancy appears. Windows close in days. The people who get through are the ones who were already practising when the advert went up.
  • Ignoring the medical until the end. Colour vision, in particular, is worth thirty pounds and an afternoon to check before you start.
  • Applying to a depot you cannot get to at 03:50. Not on a train, because there are none. In your own car, in the dark, in February.

Is it the job you think it is?

It is paid well, it is secure, and it is genuinely skilled. It is also solitary, shift-based and unforgiving of inattention. You will work nights, weekends and Christmases, book on before the first train and finish after the last, and spend hours alone watching a railway that is doing nothing unusual — until the one moment it is, which is what the whole selection process is quietly testing for.

If that appeals rather than deters, the next thing to read is the eligibility checklist, and the next thing to do is sit one full-length test and find out where you actually are.
