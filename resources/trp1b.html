TRP1b: reading rules once and keeping them

Three minutes with a passage of procedure, then it is taken away and you answer eighteen questions on it. Everything depends on how you spend those three minutes.

gold Stage 1 8 min read

Trainability for Rules and Procedures is not a memory test in the party-trick sense. It is a prediction: you are about to spend months being handed rule books, and the operator wants to know whether procedure sticks to you. Some versions include an audio section; the variant without it is sometimes called RAAT. The technique is identical.

Study
About three minutes with the passage
Questions
Around eighteen, passage removed
Clock
Commonly seven minutes, ending hard
Scoring
Correct answers; guessing is usually not punished

Read for structure, not for words

People read the passage like a page of a novel, front to back, at a comfortable pace, and get about two thirds of the way through. Then the questions sample the whole thing evenly and the last third is a blank.

Read it as a machine instead. A procedure passage is almost always the same handful of parts:

  • Actors — the driver, the signaller, the guard, control. Who does what to whom.
  • Triggers — the "if" and "when" that start a procedure.
  • Actions, in order — first, then, before, after.
  • Numbers — minutes, metres, times, counts, how many attempts.
  • Exceptions — unless, except, other than, does not apply when.

Questions are drawn overwhelmingly from the last two. Numbers and exceptions are where a passage can be tested unambiguously, so that is where the items come from.

Three minutes, spent deliberately

  1. First twenty seconds — map it. Do not read. Count the paragraphs or numbered clauses, note the headings, see how many distinct procedures are described. You now know how much you have to cover and can pace yourself against it.
  2. Next two minutes — one pass, converting as you go. Turn each clause into a sentence in your own words: if the signal fails, the driver contacts the signaller before moving. Rules stored as your own sentences survive; rules stored as remembered phrasing do not.
  3. Attack the numbers on the way past. Every number gets said to yourself twice and attached to its thing — "three attempts, three attempts" — because "three" on its own is worthless twelve questions later.
  4. Final twenty to thirty seconds — sweep the exceptions. Go back over the passage looking only for unless, except, only if, does not apply. These are the items you will otherwise get wrong while feeling confident.

Do not try to memorise verbatim. Nobody can hold a page of prose in three minutes, and the questions do not require it. What you need is the chain: trigger, actor, order, number, exception.

Drill it: TRP1b

Three minutes with the passage, then it goes and the eighteen questions start. Practice leaves those untimed so you can see what you retained; the exam version puts seven minutes on them and hands the paper in at zero.

Open the drill

Answering the questions

  • Answer from the passage, not from the railway. This trips up the people who know most. The passage is written to be self-contained and may differ from the real Rule Book on purpose. Real-world knowledge is a trap here, not an advantage.
  • Read the stem to the end. Items turn on one word — "must", "may", "before", "unless" — and a stem read at speed becomes a different question.
  • Be suspicious of absolutes. Options with "always" and "never" are frequently the distractor, because procedures are full of conditions.
  • Move at about twenty seconds a question. Eighteen questions in seven minutes means you cannot dwell. Take your best answer and go; a marked-but-moved-on item can be revisited if the clock allows, a slow one cannot be un-spent.
  • Leave nothing blank unless you are told wrong answers are penalised. An eliminated-down guess is usually worth more than a gap.

Where people lose it

  • Reading front to back at reading speed. You will not finish, and the questions will sample what you did not reach.
  • Sub-vocalising every word. It limits you to speaking pace when you need scanning pace with deliberate stops.
  • Memorising the first paragraph beautifully. Depth on one part and nothing on the rest is the worst distribution of the same effort.
  • Answering from experience. Especially common in people already working on the railway. The passage is the only authority.
  • Panicking at the handover. The moment the passage is taken away feels awful for everyone. Spend the first ten seconds writing nothing and recalling the spine — actors, triggers, order, numbers, exceptions — then start.

How to practise it

  • Rotate the passages rather than re-sitting one until you know it. The skill is the reading method, and a familiar passage cannot test it.
  • Use the practice version — untimed questions — to find out what your three-minute read actually retains. Then use the exam version to find out what it retains under a clock.
  • Practise the technique on anything: a kitchen appliance manual, a safety notice at work. Three minutes, close it, write the chain out from memory.
  • If your score is fine but your timing is not, the problem is the questions, not the study. Drill the twenty-second pace specifically.
