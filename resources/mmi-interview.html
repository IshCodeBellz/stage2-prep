The interviews: MMI and DMI

There are usually two of them and they want different things. Most candidates prepare answers for the first and nothing for the second — then find out, out loud, which of their examples fall apart when pushed.

platinum Interview 11 min read

Structured driver interviews are usually competency-based and very often preceded by a pre-interview form: a handful of written examples you submit in advance. The form is not administration. It is the source material the interviewer will spend the next hour probing, and it is the single most under-prepared document in the whole process.

Most operators put you through two interviews, and they are not the same exercise. Preparing for one and being given the other is a common and avoidable way to lose this.

MMI and DMI: two interviews, not one

You will see both acronyms in your invite letters, usually without either being explained.

  • MMI — the Multi-Modal Interview. The structured interview at the assessment centre, and the one named in the rail industry standard behind the whole battery. It is part of the standardised process: it is scored against a fixed frame, it is where your situational judgement answers get followed up, and it is what the pre-interview form feeds. You will occasionally see it expanded as "multiple mini interview" — same thing, same day.
  • DMI — the Drivers' Manager Interview. A separate interview, usually later and usually with an actual driver manager from the depot you would work at, sometimes alongside an operations manager or a senior driver. It is a conventional interview with genuine back and forth, commonly up to an hour, and at most operators it is the final hurdle before the medical. A few run it the other way round.

Everything from here to the drill is about the MMI, because that is the one with a scoring sheet and a form behind it. The DMI has its own section further down, and the difference in how you prepare for the two is larger than most candidates expect.

The competencies they are actually scoring

Wording varies, but driver frameworks cover much the same ground:

  • Safety and rule-following — including challenging others and reporting yourself.
  • Concentration and working alone — staying alert, unsupervised, for hours.
  • Communication — clear, accurate, especially under pressure or by radio.
  • Working with others — handovers, depots, guards, control.
  • Dealing with pressure and the unexpected — staying calm, following procedure when it would be easier not to.
  • Reliability and self-management — timekeeping, shifts, fatigue, preparation.
  • Customer awareness — passengers exist, even from a cab.
  • Learning and development — you are about to be trained intensively.

Choosing six examples

  1. Each example must be yours and recent. "We" is the word that loses marks. If you cannot say what you did, minute by minute, it is the wrong example.
  2. Each must have depth, not drama. A dull incident you remember precisely beats a dramatic one you remember vaguely. You will be asked what exactly you said, and to whom.
  3. Spread them across the competencies. Six examples that are all "I stayed calm in a busy shift" leave half the frame unevidenced, and unevidenced is unscored.
  4. Never reuse one example for two competencies. It halves your evidence and the interviewer will notice.
  5. Avoid the rule-breaking hero story. "I cut a corner and it worked out" is disqualifying in this industry, however good the outcome. So is "I noticed a problem and sorted it myself rather than bothering anyone".
  6. Include one where you got it wrong, reported it, and changed something. Done honestly, this is the strongest single example most candidates have, and almost nobody offers one.

Structure: STAR, and the part people leave off

Situation, Task, Action, Result — with Action taking well over half the time, because that is where the evidence lives. Then add the fifth element the good candidates use: Reflection. What you learned, what you do differently now.

A competent answer runs roughly ninety seconds to two minutes. Under two sentences is thin; over three minutes is where interviewers stop taking notes.

Surviving the probing

Assume every example gets pushed two or three levels. The patterns are predictable:

  • "What exactly did you say?" — Have the actual words. Vague reported speech is the commonest sign of a borrowed story.
  • "Why did you do that rather than X?" — Have the reason, and let it be a procedure or a safety reason rather than a preference.
  • "What would you have done if it had got worse?" — Have thought about the escalation. Who do you tell, and when do you stop and ask?
  • "What did you learn?" — Something specific that changed your behaviour, not "to always stay calm".
  • "Has that happened since?" — The check on whether the learning is real.

Prepare by writing each example out, then having somebody ask you "and then what?" four times in a row. The point at which you start inventing is the point at which the example is not strong enough for the form.

Drill it: MMI

Write your six form examples, then answer the interviewer's follow-ups out loud against the clock. It scores how much of the structure you actually covered rather than judging what you said — which is what an interviewer's tick sheet does too.

Open the drill

The DMI: the same person, a different exercise

The drivers' manager interview is not a second MMI, and the candidates who struggle with it are usually the ones who arrive with six rehearsed STAR answers and nothing else. The MMI asks whether you can evidence a competency. The DMI asks something blunter: would I put this person in a cab on my depot?

What changes:

  • The questions open out. Expect "tell me about a time" alongside "what do you know about us", "why this depot", "what do you think the hardest part of the job is". There is no fixed frame you can map your examples onto in advance.
  • It is a conversation. You are expected to ask questions and to have opinions. Silence where an MMI assessor would simply move on reads badly here.
  • They are assessing risk, not evidence. A driver manager carries the consequences of hiring you. Reliability, honesty, attitude to rules when nobody is watching, and whether you have understood what the roster will do to your life — that is what is being weighed.
  • Company knowledge counts. Know the operator: routes, traction, depots, recent news, who they are owned by, what happened to their last timetable change. This is the one part of the process where homework about the company is directly scored.
  • It is often the last gate. At most operators the DMI sits after the assessment centre and before the medical, so a pass here is close to an offer. At some — GTR among them — it comes earlier in the order. Read the invite.

Prepare for it separately: an evening on the operator, an honest answer about the shifts, and three or four questions you actually want answered. Your MMI examples still work, but they are now supporting material rather than the whole performance.

The motivation questions

"Why do you want to be a train driver?" is asked everywhere — in the MMI as a scored item and in the DMI as the question the whole conversation hangs off — and answered badly almost everywhere. Three things make an answer credible:

  • Evidence you have found out what the job is. Shift patterns, nights, Sundays, route learning, months of training, the isolation, the responsibility. Name the downsides specifically — it is the fastest way to show you are not romanticising it.
  • Something you have actually done to investigate: talked to drivers, read the training structure, looked at what a link is, understood what a depot start at 04:00 means for your household.
  • A reason that survives the follow-up. "I like trains" invites "so why not a job where you can look at them?" Responsibility, safety-critical work, independence and procedure suit some people genuinely — say which of those is you and why, with an example.

Where people lose it

  • A thin form. Everything downstream comes from it. Spend hours on it, not twenty minutes.
  • "We" instead of "I". The single most common scoring leak in competency interviews.
  • Rambling. Practise out loud with a clock. Two minutes is longer than you think and much shorter than you will use.
  • Safety-negative stories told proudly. Shortcuts, unreported incidents, covering for someone, driving tired. Any of these can end it on their own.
  • No questions at the end, or questions about pay and rosters only. Ask about the training, the routes, the link structure. This costs you little in the MMI and a great deal in the DMI, where not asking anything reads as not really wanting it.
  • Preparing for one interview and being given the other. Six polished STAR answers will not carry a DMI, and an easy chat about the company will not evidence a single MMI competency. Find out which you are sitting, and when.
  • Never having said it out loud. Reading your examples is not rehearsal. The first time your mouth makes the words should not be in the room.

The week before

  • Write the six. Cut two. Rewrite the remaining four and find two better ones.
  • Record yourself answering each, and listen back once. It is unpleasant and it is the fastest correction you will get.
  • Have somebody probe you, including someone who will not be kind about it.
  • Re-read your own form the night before, not new material. The interviewer is holding it.
  • If it is the DMI, spend an evening on the operator instead — routes, traction, depots, recent news — and write down the questions you want to ask them.
