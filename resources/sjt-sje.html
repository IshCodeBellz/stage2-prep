Situational judgement: ranking and rating

Four responses to put in order, or a scale to rate them against. It looks like a personality test and it is not — there is a key, and the key is a safety culture written down.

gold Stage 2 9 min read

Two formats, same underlying judgement. The ranking exercise gives you a situation and four possible responses to put in order of preference, usually with a tight clock — forty-five seconds is common. The rating exercise gives you responses to score individually on a scale, across many more scenarios, usually untimed.

The hierarchy that decides most items

Almost every item resolves against the same order of priorities. Learn it as an order, not as a list, and apply it before you have any opinions:

  1. Immediate safety. Stop the harm, make the situation safe, protect passengers, colleagues and the public. Nothing outranks this and nothing is worth delaying it for.
  2. The rule or procedure. Do what the rules say, even when a faster route to the same outcome is obvious. Deviating for a good reason is still deviating.
  3. Tell the right person, now. Signaller, controller, supervisor — whoever the procedure names. Prompt, accurate reporting beats a clever unilateral fix every time.
  4. Service and passengers. Delay, information, comfort. Real, and always below the three above.
  5. Everything about you. Convenience, looking capable, not wanting to bother anyone, not wanting to drop a colleague in it. Always last.

Responses that are nearly always wrong

  • Waiting to see if it resolves itself. Passivity in a safety scenario is the worst available answer, more often than not.
  • Fixing it yourself outside your authority. Feels proactive, reads as unsafe. You are not authorised, you do not have the full picture, and you have now hidden a fault.
  • Covering for a colleague. Loyalty is not a defence. An unreported safety-critical issue — fatigue, a near miss, a substance concern — is the item's whole point.
  • Confronting someone aggressively, or going over their head before speaking to them, when the situation is not urgent.
  • Deferring a safety matter to the end of the shift. Anything with "I'd mention it later" in it is fourth of four.
  • Prioritising the service over the risk. "Carry on to avoid a delay" is almost always the trap option, and it is written to be tempting.

Ranking: the technique

  1. Find the safety-critical element of the scenario first, in one line, before reading the responses. What could hurt someone here?
  2. Pick the best and the worst. These are usually obvious and they are the easiest marks on the paper. Lock them in.
  3. Now order the middle two. This is where the marks actually go, and the tie-breaker is nearly always: which one reports sooner, or which one keeps you inside procedure? Immediate-and-correct beats thorough-but-delayed.
  4. Commit and move. Forty-five seconds does not allow a re-read, and your first application of the hierarchy is more reliable than your second-guess.
  5. Answer as the job, not as yourself. Not what you would do at your current workplace — what a safety-critical employee in a rules-based industry is expected to do.

Rating: the different trap

Rating scales are untimed, which sounds easier and introduces its own failure: inconsistency. The key is looking at your whole pattern, not each item alone.

  • Use the whole scale. Rating everything 3 or 4 tells the scorer nothing, and "nothing" is not a neutral result.
  • Do not rate two contradictory responses highly. If "report it immediately" is a 5, then "deal with it yourself and mention it later" cannot also be a 4. Contradictions are detectable and they are exactly what the exercise is checking.
  • Rate the response, not the outcome. An action that happened to work out is still a poor response if it was unsafe.
  • Extremes are allowed. Genuinely dangerous responses deserve a 1. Reluctance to use the bottom of the scale is a real and common leak.
Drill it: M7-SJT and SJE

Ranking at forty-five seconds a situation with four rotating sets, and the rating exercise that checks your answers against each other for contradictions. The scenarios are set on the railway — depot, platform, cab and control — because generic office scenarios train the wrong instincts.

Open the drills

Should you just tell them what they want to hear?

Partly, and be honest with yourself about it. These exercises are not lie detectors and the "right" answers are not secret — they are the published safety behaviours of the industry. Working out what a safe railway expects and answering that way is legitimate preparation.

Where it goes wrong is over-egging. Candidates who have decided that the most dramatic safety response always wins will rank "stop everything and call control" top on an item about a passenger asking for directions. The hierarchy is an order of priority, not a volume knob: proportion is part of judgement, and some scenarios genuinely are about customer service.

How to practise it

  • Do not memorise answers. The sets rotate and the keys are not public. Drill the hierarchy until you can apply it in ten seconds.
  • After each item, say out loud why your top choice is top. If the reason is not one of the five priorities, you guessed.
  • Work the ranking exercise against the clock and the rating exercise slowly — that is how they come.
  • Read your operator's published values and safety statements. The key was written by people who use that vocabulary, and it shows.
