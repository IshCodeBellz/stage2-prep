DFFT: fault finding against a guide

Twenty questions in forty-five minutes. It is the most generous clock of the day, and the only test where slowing down reliably scores better.

gold Stage 1 7 min read

You are given a display panel showing indications — lights, readings, states — and a fault finder guide that maps combinations of indications to fault codes and the action each one requires. The question is which fault to deal with first, and what to do about it.

Items
Around twenty
Clock
Commonly forty-five minutes
Per item
Just over two minutes
Materials
A panel and a reference guide, both in front of you

What it is measuring

Not mechanical knowledge. You are not expected to know anything about the equipment, and the guide contains everything needed. What is being measured is whether you can work systematically through a reference document under mild time pressure without taking shortcuts — which is exactly what a driver does with a fault book at two in the morning with a train full of people behind them.

The test is therefore designed so that intuition gives the wrong answer often enough to catch people who trust it. The most alarming-looking indication is frequently not the highest priority one. The obvious fault is frequently a symptom of another.

The method

  1. Read the whole panel before the guide. Thirty seconds, listing every indication that is not normal. Every one. The most common error in this test is answering a question about the first abnormality you noticed while a second one sits unread.
  2. Take the indications to the guide, not the other way round. Find the row that matches the full set of what you observed. A row that matches most of your indications is not your row.
  3. Check priority explicitly. When more than one fault is live, the guide, not your judgement, decides which comes first. If the guide gives an order, use it as written even if it feels wrong.
  4. Read the action off the same row as the code. Sliding a line while moving between columns is a mundane, frequent, entirely avoidable way to get an item wrong. Put a finger or the cursor on the row.
  5. Verify once, in one direction. Take your chosen code and check backwards that its conditions are all present on the panel. One pass. If it holds, commit.

That whole cycle takes ninety seconds with practice, inside a two-minute budget. Use the slack; it is there deliberately and finishing early buys you nothing.

Drill it: DFFT

The exam version is the full twenty in forty-five minutes and ends at zero. The practice version is five with no clock — use it to build the cycle before you time it.

Where people lose it

  • Answering from mechanical intuition. If you have a trade background this is your main risk. The guide is the authority, including where it contradicts how the real thing would behave.
  • Stopping at the first abnormal indication. Read the whole panel first, every time, even when the answer looks obvious after two seconds.
  • Partial matches. A guide row that covers three of your four indications is the distractor, and it is placed there on purpose.
  • Column slip. Right fault, wrong action, because the eye moved a row while crossing the table.
  • Rushing because the other tests were rushed. You arrive at DFFT with the pace of Group Bourdon still in you. Consciously change gear.

How to practise it

  • Practise the cycle, not the content: panel sweep, full match, priority, same-row read, one verification. Say the five steps out loud until they are boring.
  • Time yourself per item rather than overall. If you are averaging well under two minutes and getting items wrong, you are skipping the sweep.
  • When you get one wrong, work out which of the five steps you skipped. It will be one of them, and it will usually be the same one each time.
  • Sit the full twenty at least once before the day. Forty-five minutes of careful table-reading is more tiring than it sounds, and the last five items are where the errors live.
